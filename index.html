Gene Kim — historian of modern Korea and Japan

Bio

I am a historian of modern Korea and Japan. Currently I am an Academy Scholar at The Harvard Academy for International and Area Studies.

I received my PhD in History and East Asian Languages in the Department of East Asian Languages and Civilizations at Harvard University in 2026. My dissertation, “Upwellings of Oceanic Decolonization: Coastal Governance in Japan and Korea, 1894–1958,” was awarded the Richard J. Samuels Dissertation Award.

Accepted at Journal of Korean Studies.

“Yukchi wa haeyang ŭi kwan’kye rŭl chungjaehagi: Hamgyŏngdo myŏngt’ae ŏŏp nodong kwa haeyang yŏngt’ohwa [Mediating land and sea: labor in the Hamgyŏng Province pollock fishery and oceanic territorialization].”

Yŏksa pip’yŏng, Vol. 146, Spring 2024 (in Korean).

Abstract

Following Alison Bashford’s conceptualization of “terraqueous history”, this article examines the process by which spaces become terraqueous and thus ripe for analysis by historians, focusing on the pollock fishery in late Chosŏn to the colonial period. This article shows how in the case of Hamgyŏng Province coastlines, it was fishermen’s labor, embodied knowledge, and markets that intensively linked land and sea from at least the end of the 17th century, establishing a large scale pollock fishery. After colonization, colonial management of the pollock fishery invited projections of imperial power onto littoral spaces and furthered oceanic territorialization. Colonialist discourse on the pollock fishery represented a claim to mediating and developing terraqueous space but made abstract the labor and embodied knowledge on which it relied. Projections of power onto littoral spaces, however, engendered further social conflict that in turn required greater assertion of control over land and sea.

Keywords: Terraqueous history; pollock; Hamgyŏng Province; labor; Japanese colonial period; oceanic territorialization.
